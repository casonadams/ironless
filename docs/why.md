# Why ironless works the way it does

ironless is meant to be easy to keep using and to build muscle with nothing but dumbbells, the floor and some
standing room. Each section below covers one idea: what the research found, how strong that finding is, and what the
app does about it. The Progress tab's **Why** view shows a short version of each.

All sources were checked against their original abstracts. Where a rule comes from coaching practice or judgment
rather than a study, it says so.

## Train each muscle about twice a week

**Evidence.** A meta-analysis of training frequency found that training a muscle group twice a week grew more muscle
than once a week, in studies where the total sets were the same (Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2016). A later, larger
meta-analysis found that when weekly sets are equal, frequency does not meaningfully change growth; studies that did
not equate sets favored higher frequency, modestly (Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019). Read together: weekly sets are
what count, and spreading them over two sessions is an easy way to get enough good ones.

**In ironless.** Push / pull / legs rotates through five training days a week, so each muscle comes round about 1.7
times a week. The app suggests the next day, and choosing another simply continues the rotation from the one you did.

## More weekly sets build more muscle

**Evidence.** A dose-response meta-analysis found that each extra weekly set was linked with a little more growth.
Grouped as fewer than 5, 5 to 9, and 10 or more sets per muscle, the trend favored more sets but fell short of
statistical significance (Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017). A review of trained young men found 12 to 20 weekly
sets a sound recommendation, with no clear difference between moderate and high volume for the quadriceps and biceps,
and an advantage for more volume in the triceps (Baz-Valle et al. 2022).

**In ironless.** The Progress tab counts the hard sets you finish for each muscle this week against a target of 10.
Ten is a sensible starting point for newer lifters, not a ceiling; the starting plan lands near it, and adding sets
or exercises moves you toward the 12 to 20 the review suggests for trained lifters.

## Light or heavy works, if sets go close to failure

**Evidence.** A meta-analysis comparing light loads (60% of one-rep max or less) with heavy loads, with every set
taken to muscular failure, found similar muscle growth; heavy loads built more maximal strength (Schoenfeld et al.
2017). A follow-up review argues that muscle can be built across a wide range of loads, rather than only in a
"hypertrophy rep range" (Schoenfeld et al. 2021).

**In ironless.** This is what makes home dumbbells enough. When you reach your heaviest dumbbell, the app does not
stall: it keeps asking for one more rep. The reps you log are what move you forward.

## Add reps, then add weight

**Evidence.** None directly: double progression is a widespread coaching method, not a program that has been tested
against others. It applies the findings above in a simple, repeatable way.

**In ironless.** Each exercise has a rep range, 8 to 12 for most. The next workout starts every set one rep past last
time. When every set reaches the top of the range, the app moves you up to the next dumbbell you own and back to the
bottom of the range. The hint on each exercise says which: "Beat last time: 25 lb x 10, 9, 9" or "Top of the range
last time: move up to 30 lb".

## A missed day is not a broken habit

**Evidence.** In a 12-week study, 96 people picked a daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do in the same
context each day. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect how the habit formed. The time to reach 95% of
each person's peak automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days (Lally et al. 2010). The study tracked daily habits, not
workouts, so it supports forgiveness more than it sets a schedule.

**In ironless.** There is no daily streak to break. The goal is a number of workouts a week (5 by default), and a
skipped day only means the rotation waits for you.

## Train for your own reasons

**Evidence.** A cross-sectional study of 2,771 users of one fitness app found that people with autonomous motivation,
training because they enjoy it or value it, stayed with the app longer (Fuente-Vidal et al. 2026). It shows an
association, not a cause, and it is one app.

**In ironless.** The app shows real progress (best sets, weekly sets per muscle, what to beat) and lets you choose and
swap exercises. It has no badges, points or guilt messages.

## What floor and standing gives up

**Evidence.** None: these are practical limits of the equipment, not study findings.

- **Chest**: the floor stops a press short, so the chest is stretched less than on a bench. Floor flies and push-ups
  (which can be swapped in) help.
- **Lats**: without a bar there is no pulling from overhead. Rows and floor pullovers cover much of it; a doorway
  pull-up bar would cover the rest.
- **Legs**: legs outgrow light dumbbells quickly. Single-leg moves (split squats, reverse lunges, single-leg Romanian
  deadlifts) make the same weight harder, and higher reps keep progress going, as the loading research allows.
